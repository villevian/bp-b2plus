Part 1 · Why the best founders talk to users · 00:42–03:23 ▶ watch on YouTube
Listen for: throughout the lifetime of the companykeep you honestfigure out who to talk toreach out todon’t be afraid of rejectionoutside your personal circlesbuild personal connections with
  1. When do great founders start talking to users? Before they even have a product — and they keep learning from users throughout the life of the company.
  2. Why do users and customers “keep you honest”? They are the only stakeholders who actually pay you, so they will tell you the truth.
  3. Who are the only stakeholders who actually pay the company? Users and customers.
Part 2 · How to interview · 05:58–09:42 ▶ watch on YouTube
Listen for: feel comfortableopen upopen-ended questionstake notesbias someone’s answerslisten, not talkdive deep intoderail the interviewconcrete and real examples
  1. What is the best format for a user interview? A video or phone call (or in person).
  2. Why shouldn’t you pitch your idea at the start? You’ll get polite reactions to your idea instead of real information about the user’s problems.
  3. What is your role in the interview? To listen, not to talk — and to ask open-ended follow-up questions.
Part 3 · Questions to ask and to avoid · testing a prototype · 09:42–17:04 ▶ watch on YouTube
Listen for: focus on features instead of problemsover-intellectualisebucket ideas into categoriesbe in the spacea formidable competitormove someone off (a tool)notoriously difficultopen to trying new thingsclick through a prototypekeep interviewees involvedmake them feel special
  1. Which types of questions should you avoid? Yes/no questions, “Would you use this?” and “Which features do you want?”
  2. What is the “one big danger” during interviews? Focusing on features instead of problems.
  3. What does he mean by “good problems, bad solutions”? Users know their problems well, but their ideas for solutions are often bad. Finding the solution is your job.
  4. How can you tell that a problem is valuable? People already pay for solutions to it; free tools like spreadsheets aren’t strong competitors; the audience is easy to sell to.
  5. What should you do while a user clicks through your prototype? Watch without giving instructions and ask them to think aloud.
  6. How can you keep interviewees involved after the research? For example, in a shared group chat: share your progress and make them feel special.
Part 1 · A direct connection to users · 00:27–02:43 ▶ watch on YouTube
Listen for: maintain a direct connectionat all different stagesoutsource the researchthe go-betweenpeople skillseasier said than doneone of the critical factorsat the core
  1. What do the best founders do throughout the life of their company? They maintain a direct connection to their users.
  2. What are the two things founders should do, according to the YC motto? Write code and talk to users.
Part 2 · Three common mistakes · 02:43–05:56 ▶ watch on YouTube
Listen for: pitch your ideasell someone on somethingin the first placetalk about hypotheticalscompany-changing decisionshard data
  1. What are the three common mistakes? Talking about your idea instead of the user’s life; asking about hypotheticals instead of specifics; talking instead of listening.
  2. Why are hypothetical questions dangerous? People are too optimistic about the future. Their answers don’t show what they will actually do.
Part 3 · Five great questions · 05:56–14:16 ▶ watch on YouTube
Listen for: the hardest part about …put yourself back in the position of …pain pointson a regular basiswarrant solvingend up duplicating workget at the root ofa burning problemroll out a solutionfeature set
  1. What are the five questions? What’s the hardest part about…? When was the last time you encountered…? Why was it hard? What have you done to solve it? What don’t you love about the solutions you’ve tried?
  2. Which question helps you find out if the problem is real? “What have you done to solve it?” If they haven’t tried anything, the problem may not be painful enough.
  3. Why does he ask about the last time, not “usually”? A specific real event gives you facts; “usually” gives you general and often inaccurate answers.
